# Certification & Training Hub

> **Quick Answer:** Certification proves *competence* (a professional signal issued by industry bodies like ALOA). A license proves *legal permission to operate* (a government mandate). The two are different things, and confusing them is the single most common mistake people make when entering the trade.

## What This Hub Covers

This hub is the highest-leverage E-E-A-T asset on the site: it helps real people enter a trade that is simultaneously **unlicensed in most places** and **criminalized-by-possession in a few**. We keep three questions cleanly separated, because the industry routinely blurs them:

| Question | Answered by | Type of law |
|---|---|---|
| Am I *allowed to practice* locksmithing for hire here? | State/county/city licensing | Occupational (civil/administrative) |
| Is *possessing* the tools legal here? | Possession statutes (intent vs prima facie) | Criminal |
| Am I *good enough* to be trusted? | ALOA / MLA certification | Voluntary / industry |

If you read only one thing, read the [How to Become a Locksmith](how-to-become-a-locksmith.md) pillar. It walks the 5-step path and explains the license-vs-certification split in full.

## The Three-Category US Framework (License / Intent / Prima Facie)

US law on locksmith tools sorts into three categories that people constantly mix up:

1. **License states** — 13 states require a license/registration to *practice* locksmithing for hire (AL, CA, CT, IL, LA, MD, NV, NJ, NC, OK, OR, TX, VA). This is about *working*, not *owning tools*.
2. **Intent states** — the majority. Possessing picks is legal; the state must prove you *intended* to commit a crime (e.g., burglary). Minnesota §609.59 is the textbook example.
3. **Prima facie states** — a small set (MS, VA, and commonly-cited NV/OH) where possession itself is treated as *presumptive evidence* of intent, and the burden shifts to you to show a lawful purpose.

The full matrix, with statute citations, lives in [Locksmith License by State](locksmith-license-by-state.md).

## A Note on Accuracy

Licensing lists **change**. Some states sunset licenses (Illinois plans 2029), two states already repealed theirs (Nebraska and Tennessee in 2021), and some counties carry the load where the state does not (Miami-Dade and Hillsborough in Florida; NYC and Nassau County in New York). We mark source dates, cite the underlying regulator, and flag the "13 vs 15 states" discrepancy honestly rather than publishing a frozen, wrong list.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** I'd rather a reader learn the *framework* — license vs certification, and license vs intent vs prima facie — than memorize any state list. Lists rot; the framework is stable.

**Reasoning:** 1) Every "which states require a license" article on the web is out of date the month after it's written, because licensing is being *rolled back* faster than it's added. 2) The genuinely dangerous confusion isn't "do I need a license," it's "is my tool legal to own here" — a criminal question that a career guide never asks. 3) The certification ladder (ALOA) is the only part of this whole field that's stable and portable across state lines, which is exactly why it's the part worth investing in first.

**Disclosure:** This is my editorial judgment based on primary sources (ALOA advocacy, state statutes, state regulator pages) reviewed September 2026 — not legal advice, and not a substitute for checking your own jurisdiction before you practice or carry tools.
