## Quick Answer

Professional locksmith equipment is best understood as a **task-driven system, not a pile of tools**. This hub classifies every tool by function — from picks and tension wrenches to key cutting machines and automotive programmers — and maps each one to the lock types it works on, the maintenance it needs, and the risk grade (A/B/C) it carries under our Legal Risk Matrix (document 01).

---

## Definition

**Locksmith tools** are the specialized instruments a licensed or credentialed locksmith uses to install, repair, service, decode, and re-key locks and vehicle key systems. In this hub they are organized by **what task they perform**, not by their physical shape.

## Tool ↔ Lock-Type Quick Reference

| Tool family | Primary lock types it works on | Does **not** work on |
|---|---|---|
| Hook / rake picks | Pin tumbler | Disc detainer, dimple, electronic |
| Ball pick | Wafer | Pin tumbler (less precise) |
| Pick gun (snap / EPG) | Pin tumbler (standard) | Disc detainer, dimple, smart |
| Lishi 2-in-1 | Keyway-matched (pin/wafer) | Unmatched keyways |
| Key cutting machine | All mechanical (edge + high-security) | N/A (mechanical cuts only) |
| Key programmer | Transponder/smart key vehicles | Non-electronic vehicles |

## Responsible Use

Categories here carry legal sensitivity in certain jurisdictions. We grade each tool A/B/C per our Legal Risk Matrix (document 01) and frame B/C-class tools (lock picks, decoders, Lishi, automotive programmers) with **licensed/authorized-professional context** — never with circumvention or "how to open" step-by-step guidance.

## How This Hub Works

This is the closest hub to commerce: tool knowledge naturally links to product selection. But we prioritize **honest evaluation** over selling — including calling out when a tool is overrated (e.g., tubular picks) or has hidden costs (e.g., automotive programmer subscriptions).

---

## The Author's Take

**My position: a locksmith's toolkit should be built from the task list backwards, not from a catalog forwards.** The three decisions that actually determine whether a tool earns its keep are (1) *which lock types your service area actually contains*, (2) *whether the tool's risk grade fits your jurisdiction's licensing*, and (3) *its true lifetime cost including calibration and consumables* — not its marketing "opens anything" claim. Buying the full kit first is the most expensive way to learn that most of it stays in the drawer.

(Reasoning: task-driven selection matches how working locksmiths actually build kits; risk grade is a hard compliance ceiling in most jurisdictions; consumable and subscription costs are the hidden second price of many tools. This is editorial judgment, not verified fact.)
