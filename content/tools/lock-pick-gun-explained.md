## Quick Answer

A **lock pick gun** (also called a snap gun / pick pistol) is a tool that operates through **energy transfer**: a thin steel needle is positioned against the bottom pins of a pin tumbler lock and, when triggered, **strikes all the bottom pins simultaneously**, transferring momentum to the driver pins so they momentarily clear the shear line — while a tension wrench turns the plug.

Two types:

| Type | Power | Characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| **Manual snap gun** | Trigger, spring-driven single "snap" | Slower, manual triggering per attempt |
| **Electric pick gun (EPG)** | Motor-driven, continuous rapid vibration | Fast, low-effort, working-locksmith standard |

**Key limitation**: a pick gun **works only on standard pin tumbler locks** — not on disc detainer, dimple, or electronic/smart locks.

## Definition

A **lock pick gun** is a mechanical or electric tool that uses a striking needle to deliver a burst of kinetic energy into the pin stack of a **pin tumbler lock**. It is an **automated relative of key bumping**: where bumping strikes a specially-cut key by hand, the pick gun uses a spring (snap gun) or motor (electric pick gun) to produce the strike more controllably.

---

## History

The pick gun is older than most people assume:

- **1934** — Louis S. Hanflig patented the first snap gun in the United States.
- **1943** — Segal Samuel patented a "Lock Picking Gun" (US Patent 2,309,677).
- **1951** — William J. Miskill patented a "Power Actuated Lock Pick" (US Patent 2,565,254).

The **manual snap gun** was later popularized as a simple, non-destructive tool for US police forces requiring little training. The **electric pick gun (EPG)** is the motor-driven improvement of that same idea. Representative modern models include the **Brockhage Manual Pick Gun**, the **KLOM EPG**, the **Dino Li-ion EPG**, and the **Multipick Kronos EPG**.

## The Working Principle: Energy Transfer, Not Single-Pin Precision

Where single-pin picking is "trial-and-error, one pin at a time," the pick gun relies on a physics principle — **momentum transfer**:

- The needle rests against the bottom pins (the "key pins").
- On trigger, the needle strikes, and — like a cue ball hitting a target ball — the bottom pins transfer kinetic energy to the driver pins above them.
- The driver pins gain momentum and momentarily rise past the shear line while the heavier bottom pins stay relatively in place.
- With light rotational torque applied at that instant, the plug is free to turn.

This is the same underlying phenomenon as **bumping**: striking a key sends the driver pins upward for a brief window. The pick gun's needle-and-spring (or motor) simply automates the strike. *(Described here at the principle level only — this page does not provide usage instruction.)*

## Its Strengths and Weaknesses (Said Honestly)

**Strengths**:
- **Fast** — on low-to-medium security pin tumbler locks, usually seconds to tens of seconds.
- **Low barrier** — originally designed for US police, requiring "non-destructive opening without long training."

**Weaknesses**:
- **Pin tumbler only** — disc detainer, dimple, and electronic locks don't apply.
- **More damaging** — the strike is more damaging to the lock mechanism than raking (which simulates key movement). Wikipedia explicitly notes this.
- **Weak against security pins** — on locks with spool/serrated pins, the driver pins catch on false positions after jumping, sharply lowering success.
- **Often needs multiple attempts** — not a one-trigger open; usually requires repeated triggering.

## Tool ↔ Lock-Type Compatibility

| Lock type | Pick gun works? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Standard pin tumbler (Kwikset/Weiser/Schlage-class) | ✔ | Straight pin stacks transfer energy cleanly |
| Pin tumbler with security pins | ⚠ Partial | Spool/serrated pins catch on false sets |
| Paracentric / narrow keyway (some European/Yale) | ⚠ Partial | Needle can't reach pins squarely |
| Wafer (automotive) | ✘ | Flat wafers, different geometry |
| Disc detainer | ✘ | No pins to strike |
| Dimple | ✘ | Different pin orientation |
| Electronic / smart | ✘ | No mechanical pin stack |

## How It Relates to Common Techniques

| Technique | Principle | Relationship to pick gun |
|---|---|---|
| Single-pin picking (SPP) | Lift pins one at a time, precisely | The pick gun's "slow, precise" contrast |
| Raking | Quickly sweep multiple pins | Closer to natural key motion, less damaging than a pick gun |
| Bumping | Strike a special key by hand | The pick gun is its automated version |

Key insight: **pick gun = automated bumping**, inheriting bumping's "fast + damaging + weak against security pins" triplet.

## Maintenance & Failure Modes

- **Failure mode 1 — worn/bent needle**: the striking needle deforms with use and stops striking squarely; needles are consumable and sold as replacements.
- **Failure mode 2 — battery/dead EPG**: an electric gun with a flat battery is the "no petrol" failure — charge before jobs.
- **Failure mode 3 — weak spring (snap gun)**: a fatigued trigger spring reduces strike force; adjust or replace per model.
- **Maintenance**: keep the needle clean and straight, charge EPGs regularly, and store with the needle protected.

## The Author's Take

**My position: the pick gun is a "fast but rough" tool, suited to specific contexts (emergency access / bulk low-security pin tumbler locks), but not a "high-quality locksmith's" first choice.** Three points:

1. **It's a different thing from "professional picking technique"** — the pick gun relies on energy transfer and "getting lucky," while single-pin picking relies on feel and precise positioning. Against high-security locks, the former fails and the latter is the correct path.
2. **"Lock damage" is a real cost to factor in** — the strike can damage the lock mechanism. That may be unacceptable on a client's front door (it's more acceptable on locksport practice locks).
3. **It's been partly superseded by tool evolution** — the electric EPG upgrades the manual snap gun, but true professionals still return to single-pin picking and dedicated tools (like Lishi) for high-security locks.

(Reasoning: energy transfer is inherently less controllable than feel-based SPP; impact wear is a documented downside versus raking; the EPG is a convenience upgrade, not a capability upgrade. This is professional judgment based on pick-gun public principles and locksmith industry consensus, not empirical testing.)

## FAQ

**Can a pick gun open all locks?**
No. It works only on standard pin tumbler locks — disc detainer, dimple, and electronic/smart locks don't apply.

**What's the difference between manual and electric pick guns?**
Manual triggers once per pull; electric (EPG) is motor-driven continuous vibration — faster and lower-effort.

**How does a pick gun relate to bumping?**
A pick gun is essentially an automated version of bumping, using a spring/motor instead of hand strikes.

**Does a pick gun damage locks?**
Yes, and more than raking (striking vs. simulated key movement).

**Who was the first to patent a snap gun?**
Louis S. Hanflig, in 1934; later designs followed from Segal Samuel (1943) and William J. Miskill (1951).
