## Quick Answer

A lock pick is not a single "universal key" — it's a **class of differently-shaped manipulation tools**, each aimed at different locks and different techniques. It splits into two core families: **single-pin picking (SPP) tools** (hook, half-diamond) and **raking tools** (city rake, Bogota, snake). What actually makes every pick work is the **tension wrench** — it applies rotational torque, holds pins at the shear line, and delivers the tactile feedback. Pick selection is dominated by two variables: **tip shape** (what technique it enables) and **thickness** (which keyway it fits).

> ⚠️ Compliance note: this content is **tool classification and mechanism education only**, not usage instruction. Tool possession and use carry legal restrictions in most jurisdictions (see our Legal Risk Matrix, document 01).

## Definition

A **lock pick** is a thin, shaped probe used by a licensed or credentialed locksmith to manipulate the tumbler components of a lock during lawful entry and re-key work. A **tension wrench** is the companion tool that applies rotational torque to the plug and relays tactile feedback. The two are inseparable: the pick positions the pins, the wrench controls and reads the result.

## What Each Pick Type Does

### Hook Pick

The most common pick, also called the "feeler" or "finger." **Used only for single-pin picking, never for raking.** The hooked tip lifts one pin without disturbing its neighbors — the professional's first choice for "traditional picking." A short hook + a medium hook cover most common pin tumbler locks.

### Half-Diamond Pick

A triangular-tipped "universal hybrid." Precise enough for single-pin picking (tight/odd keyways), angled enough to rake as a mini-rake. The entry-set "all-rounder." Industry rule: if you keep only two picks, it's **hook + half-diamond**.

### Ball Pick

Semi-circular / round tip. **Dedicated to wafer locks**, because wafer tumblers are flat and a ball tip pushes them flush more smoothly.

### Rake Pick

Uses a "sweeping" motion to set multiple pins at once. Common types: **city rake**, **Bogota**, **snake**. Highly effective on low-security, loose-tolerance locks, but weak against high-security locks (especially those with security pins).

### Comb Pick

Lifts all pins to the highest position at once — mechanically distinct from single-pin picking.

### Decoder Pick

Reads pin depths while picking, useful for key-cutting and lock analysis.

## Pick ↔ Lock-Type Compatibility

| Pick type | Technique | Best lock type | Weak against |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short/medium hook | SPP | Pin tumbler (standard + security pins) | — |
| Half-diamond | SPP / light rake | Pin tumbler, odd keyways | High-security pins |
| Ball | SPP | Wafer | Pin tumbler (imprecise) |
| City rake / Bogota / snake | Raking | Low-security pin tumbler | Security pins, tight tolerance |
| Comb | Lift-all | Low-security pin tumbler | Deep-cut / high-security |
| Decoder | Read | Pin tumbler (for code reading) | — |

## Steel & Thickness: The Two Hidden Specs

Pick performance is governed less by brand than by **material** and **thickness**:

### Material

| Steel | Characteristics | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **1095 spring steel** | High yield, flexible, rusts without plating | Budget/handmade picks |
| **301/302 stainless (full hard)** | ~140 ksi yield, corrosion-resistant, cold-stamped | Mass-market picks |
| **17-7PH stainless** | Precipitation-hardened, high strength | Premium picks ("government steel"-class) |

### Thickness

Common commercial thicknesses are **0.015", 0.018", 0.022", and 0.025"** (≈ 0.4 mm, 0.45 mm, 0.56 mm, 0.64 mm). The selection rule from the field: **use the thickest pick that fits the keyway** — thicker picks are stiffer, give better feedback, and last longer. North American keyways generally accept 0.025"; narrower European and paracentric keyways force thinner (0.015"–0.018") profiles. Anything under ~0.012" is too limp to move pins reliably; over ~0.032" won't enter most keyways.

## The Tension Wrench: The Real Lead Character

An industry-wide consensus: **"You might open some locks with a tension wrench alone, but you'll never open one with a pick alone."** Because:

- It **applies rotational torque**, holding set pins at the shear line;
- It **relays feedback** through your fingertips — which pin is "binding," which is "set," which is "overset";
- It is the **control hub** of the entire picking process — the pick is just the probe.

### Common Types

| Type | Characteristic |
|---|---|
| **L-shape (standard)** | Simple and sturdy, fits most keyways, beginner's first choice |
| **Z-shape / double-ended** | Two thicknesses on one tool, fits narrow/wide keyways, portable |
| **TOK (top of keyway)** | Seats at the top of the keyway, stable, clear feedback |
| **BOK (bottom of keyway)** | Seats at the bottom of the keyway, most common |

Tension wrenches are typically spring steel or stainless, often sold as "flat" sets with multiple thicknesses and flex resistances (e.g., the flat-5 style). A complete set should include both TOK and BOK, in multiple thicknesses.

## Minimum Viable Beginner Set

You don't need dozens of picks to start. A reasonable minimum:

- **Short hook + medium hook** (SPP workhorses)
- **1–2 rakes** (city rake or Bogota, practice sweeping)
- **Multiple tension wrenches** (TOK + BOK, multiple thicknesses)

This set opens a large fraction of common locks and builds the core feel.

## Lifespan, Maintenance & Failure Modes

- **Failure mode 1 — tip bend/fatigue**: thin picks (0.015") bend and eventually fatigue-crack under repeated prying. Inspect tips before each job.
- **Failure mode 2 — rust**: unplated 1095 spring steel rusts in humid vans; stainless resists. Store dry.
- **Failure mode 3 — blunt tip**: a rounded tip skips off pins and destroys feedback; a worn pick reads "dead."
- **Maintenance**: wipe clean, keep dry, replace any pick with a visibly bent or kinked tip. Picks are consumables, not heirlooms.

## The Author's Take

**My position: the biggest beginner mistake with picks is "buying a 50-piece set and feeling professional," while ignoring the two variables that actually decide the outcome — the tension wrench and the lock's own security grade.**

Three points:

1. **The tension wrench matters more than the pick** — it's the center of feedback and control. Before buying picks, make sure your wrench count and thicknesses are sufficient.
2. **Pick count ≠ capability** — a basic security-pinned lock defeats dozens of picks; a low-security lock opens in seconds with one rake and one wrench. Budget steel quality and thickness coverage, not quantity.
3. **Compliance is the ceiling, not the floor** — tool-classification knowledge itself is harmless, but the "legal possession / legal use" boundary varies by country, and that line is what separates the professional from the criminal.

(Reasoning: torque is the feedback channel that picking depends on; a single correctly-chosen pick out-performs a box of wrong ones; jurisdiction is a hard gate on B-class tool work. This is professional judgment based on locksmith industry tool knowledge and each tool type's accepted purpose, not empirical testing.)

## FAQ

**What is the most common lock pick?**
The hook — the single-pin picking workhorse; a short + medium hook covers most common locks.

**What does a tension wrench do?**
Applies rotational torque, holds pins at the shear line, and relays tactile feedback. It's more central to picking than the pick itself.

**What's the difference between TOK and BOK?**
TOK seats at the top of the keyway (stable, clear feedback); BOK seats at the bottom (most common). A set should include both.

**What is a ball pick used for?**
Dedicated to wafer locks — the ball tip pushes flat wafers flush more smoothly.

**What thickness of pick should I buy?**
Use the thickest that fits your keyway — 0.025" for most North American locks; 0.015"–0.018" for narrow European/paracentric keyways.
