## Direct Answer

A lock pick is not a single "universal key" — it's a **class of differently-shaped manipulation tools**, each aimed at different locks and different techniques. It splits into two core families:

1. **Single-Pin Picking (SPP) tools** — hook, half-diamond, lifting one pin at a time with precision.
2. **Raking tools** — city rake, Bogota, snake, quickly "sweeping" low-security locks.

And what actually makes every pick work is the **tension wrench** — it applies rotational torque, holds pins at the shear line, and delivers the tactile feedback.

## What Each Pick Type Does

### Hook Pick

The most common pick, also called the "feeler" or "finger." **Used only for single-pin picking, never for raking.** The hooked tip lifts one pin without disturbing its neighbors — the professional's first choice for "traditional picking." A short hook + a medium hook cover most common locks.

### Half-Diamond Pick

A triangular-tipped "universal hybrid." Precise enough for single-pin picking (tight/odd keyways), angled enough to rake as a mini-rake. The entry-set "all-rounder." Industry rule: if you keep only two picks, it's **hook + half-diamond**.

### Ball Pick

Semi-circular / round tip. **Dedicated to wafer locks**, because wafer tumblers are flat and a ball tip pushes them flush more smoothly.

### Rake Pick

Uses a "sweeping" motion to set multiple pins at once. Common types: **city rake**, **Bogota**, **snake**. Highly effective on low-security, loose-tolerance locks, but weak against high-security locks (especially those with security pins).

### Comb Pick

Lifts all pins to the highest position at once — mechanically distinct from single-pin picking.

### Decoder Pick

Reads pin depths while picking, useful for key-cutting and lock analysis.

## The Tension Wrench: The Real Lead Character

An industry-wide consensus: **"You might open some locks with a tension wrench alone, but you'll never open one with a pick alone."** Because:

- It **applies rotational torque**, holding set pins at the shear line;
- It **relays feedback** through your fingertips — which pin is "binding," which is "set," which is "overset";
- It is the **control hub** of the entire picking process — the pick is just the probe.

### Common Types

| Type | Characteristic |
|---|---|
| **L-shape (standard)** | Simple and sturdy, fits most keyways, beginner's first choice |
| **Z-shape / double-ended** | Two thicknesses on one tool, fits narrow/wide keyways, portable |
| **TOK (top of keyway)** | Seats at the top of the keyway, stable, clear feedback |
| **BOK (bottom of keyway)** | Seats at the bottom of the keyway, most common |

A complete tension-wrench set should include both TOK and BOK, in multiple thicknesses, because keyways vary in width.

## Minimum Viable Beginner Set

You don't need dozens of picks to start. A reasonable minimum:

- **Short hook + medium hook** (SPP workhorses)
- **1–2 rakes** (city rake or Bogota, practice sweeping)
- **Multiple tension wrenches** (TOK + BOK, multiple thicknesses)

This set opens a large fraction of common locks and builds the core feel.

## The Author's Take

**My judgment: the biggest beginner mistake with picks is "buying a 50-piece set and feeling professional," while ignoring the two variables that actually decide the outcome — the tension wrench and the lock's own security grade.**

Three points:

1. **The tension wrench matters more than the pick** — it's the center of feedback and control. Before buying picks, make sure your wrench count and thicknesses are sufficient.
2. **Pick count ≠ capability** — a basic security-pinned lock defeats dozens of picks; a low-security lock opens in seconds with one rake and one wrench.
3. **Compliance is the ceiling, not the floor** — tool-classification knowledge itself is harmless, but the "legal possession / legal use" boundary varies by country, and that line is what separates the professional from the criminal.

## FAQ

**What is the most common lock pick?**
The hook — the single-pin picking workhorse; a short + medium hook covers most common locks.

**What does a tension wrench do?**
Applies rotational torque, holds pins at the shear line, and relays tactile feedback. It's more central to picking than the pick itself.

**What's the difference between TOK and BOK?**
TOK seats at the top of the keyway (stable, clear feedback); BOK seats at the bottom (most common). A set should include both.

**What is a ball pick used for?**
Dedicated to wafer locks — the ball tip pushes flat wafers flush more smoothly.
