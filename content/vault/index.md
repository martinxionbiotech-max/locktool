# Vault & Safe

## Quick Answer

A safe is rated by **two independent systems** that buyers routinely conflate: the **lock** rating (the lock's tested resistance to manipulation, radiological imaging, or electronic attack) and the **body** rating (how long the container resists forced physical entry). In North America the body is rated under **UL 1037** (residential) or **UL 687** (commercial TL/TRTL/TXTL) and locks under **UL 768 / UL 2058**. In Europe the body is rated under **EN 14450 (S1/S2)** or **EN 1143-1 (Eurograde 0–XIII)** and locks under **EN 1300 (Class A/B/C/D)**. A top-tier lock on a thin steel body is still a weak safe.

> **Definition:** A **safe (burglary-resistant container)** is a lockable container engineered to resist unauthorized entry for a defined, tested duration against a defined tool set. "Burglary rating" quantifies the container body's resistance to forced entry (prying, drilling, cutting, torching); "lock rating" quantifies the lock's resistance to non-destructive opening (manipulation, radiological imaging, or electronic attack). These are separate tests with separate standards.

## The Core Insight

Three separate things get labeled "the safe's rating," and conflating them is the most common buyer mistake:

1. **Lock rating** (UL 768 Groups / UL 2058 Type 1 / EN 1300 Class) — resistance to *manipulation, radiological imaging, and electronic attack*. It never tests the body.
2. **Body rating** (UL 1037 RSC / UL 687 TL / EN 14450 / EN 1143-1) — resistance to *physical forced entry* against a defined tool set and time budget.
3. **Fire rating** (UL 72 / EN 1047-1 / NT Fire 017) — resistance to heat, not burglary. This hub covers burglary only; do not substitute one for the other.

The two burglary systems (lock vs body) are **not interchangeable**, and the North American and European systems are **not directly comparable**. Learn the map, not the memorized numbers.

## The Two Rating Worlds at a Glance

| Region | Body standard | Lock standard | Body grades | Lock grades |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| North America | UL 1037 (residential RSC), UL 687 (commercial TL/TRTL/TXTL) | UL 768 (mechanical), UL 2058 (electronic) | RSC → TL-15 → TL-30 → TRTL → TXTL | Group 2 / 2M / 1 / 1R; Type 1 |
| Europe | EN 14450 (S1/S2), EN 1143-1 (Eurograde) | EN 1300 | S1/S2; Grade 0–XIII | Class A / B / C / D |

## The Author's Take

**In my view, the single most valuable thing a buyer can learn about safes is that "the rating" is plural — and that the European and North American systems measure different things and should never be treated as equivalent.** Three points:

1. **A safe is only as strong as its weakest rated layer.** A Group 1R lock or a Class D electronic lock buys you nothing on a 12-gauge residential body that can be pried open in minutes. Buy the lock grade and the body grade together, not separately.
2. **The "20 hours" or "Grade V" numbers describe *test conditions*, not *guaranteed survival time*.** They are comparative benchmarks against a defined tool set and a defined attacker skill level — useful for ranking, misleading if read as a stopwatch promise.
3. **For almost every residential buyer, the European EN 14450 S2 or North American RSC is the rational ceiling** — above that you are paying for resistance to professional tool attacks that a home burglar does not mount, and for insurance cash ratings most homeowners never need.
